## Important Aside/Disclaimer: Marks and "Volatility"

There's nothing necessarily illegitimate about this, per se,
and I can't even guarantee that this applies here,
but I strenuously admonish the reader against assuming
that a fund being visibly registered/listed/traded/liquid
automatically implies that its short- or even medium-term marks
are substantively transparent and/or market-based.

Imagine a hypothetical niche asset class where:
- Professional expertise and coverage are sparse
  and information edges (advantageous asymmetry)
  are jealously guarded.
  - Information lives, circulates, and propagates
    among small, remote, isolated individual tidepools.
  - Fresh information escapes containment irregularly via
    lethargic trickles or diffuse clouds,
    with only rare and sporadic (but earthshaking) floods.
- There isn't much "dumb money" interest,
  but even the sophisticated third-party
  buyers and sellers of managed fund shares
  have limited ability to form/shift
  independent views on the underlying assets,
  which are themselves highly illiquid.
  Hence, day-to-day changes in observed share transaction prices
  mainly just reflect changes in demand due to auxiliary things like
  - flows and wealth effects,
  - risk-on-vs-risk-off posturing/dynamics,
  - alternatives and opportunity costs,
  - the "Fed model"
    (which must be [fought](https://www.aqr.com/Insights/Research/Journal-Article/Fight-the-Fed-Model)),
  - plain-old animal spirits.

In this hypothetical niche asset class:
- Managers have no choice but to assign "fair valuation" marks
  with finger-in-the-air nudges every once in a while,
  and investors have no choice but to accept them.
  - Even if the manager would _like_ to be
    as radically open and straightforward as possible,
    the underlying information is latent and hidden.
    Without much to go off of most of the time,
    fair-value updates will
    tend to look deceptively stable.
  - The fact that the manager can't afford to waste time
    running a fresh valuation every day means that
    a lot of random stuff that happens between updates
    but then course-corrects or mean-reverts before the next update
    won't show up in the final numbers that go out.
  - Realistically, the manager _also_ has an obvious _incentive_
    to put a thumb on the scale to make struck NAV look even _more_ stable,
    because a boring straight line upward may be more palatable to investor appetites
    than a bumpy/spiky/wiggly line that ends up in the same place.
    - "Independent" third-party valuation agents can't save you.
      First of all, they are themselves often non-experts
      who largely defer to the manager's view,
      with some nominal modicum of adjustment/validation on top of it.
      Moreover, even if they _could_ furnish a genuine independent view,
      they know that persistently making the fund look bad
      will get them fired by the manager.
- But the underlying economic return distribution remains highly non-Normal,
  and even "nonparametric" quantile-based risk measures like VaR
  can't save you because empirical estimates are noisy and unreliable.
- The one thing we _can_ say for sure is that
  observed daily share-price return volatility
  is not a good indicator of
  either valuation uncertainty
  or stress-loss frequency/magnitude.

## Cautionary Tales

### What do AQR and PIMCO know that we don't?

- AQR
  - Tale:
    - launched AQR Re in this space around 2011
      - hired a bunch of industry veterans
      - invested a ton into spinning up operations
      - posted decent returns...
    - yet, shuttered the unit within 5 years (around 2015)
      - without a clear impetus
      - citing only a corporate-safe excuse
        about operational complexity and strategy capacity.
- Allianz/PIMCO
  - Tale:
    - launched their own ILS platform around 2019
      - *also* hired a bunch of industry veterans
      - *also* invested a ton into spinning up operations
      - *also* posted decent returns...
    - and yet, *also* shuttered their unit within 5 years (around 2023)
      - *also* without a clear impetus
      - *also* citing only a corporate-safe excuse
        about operational complexity and strategy capacity.
  - Notes:
    - The PIMCO ILS team went on to found King Ridge Capital,
      subadviser on the `TXSE:ILS` ETF from above.
    - Around 2024, Allianz/PIMCO relaunched
      two separate fresh reinsurance ventures.
      - Pando Re, where the cedant/sponsor (original insurer) is
        Aspen Insurance's casualty/liability business
        (so, in particular, *not* dealing with catastrophe/disaster).
      - Sconset Re, where the cedant/sponsor (original insurer) is
        Allianz Insurance's own life/annuity business
        (so, again, *not* catastrophe/disaster).

It's possible that
giant asset managers
whose major value prop
is synergies/economies of scale
from highly-marketable funds
running on shared firmwide
data/trading/reporting infrastructure
simply aren't effective hosts
for niche/opaque/finnicky investment strategies,
so both sides come to realize
they gain nothing from the tie-up
and hence part ways.
But it's also possible that
the big guys
are initially enthusiastic
then eventually get a sinking gut feel
as they observe the precarity
of picking up pennies
in front of a steamroller.
Which of these scenarios
is closer to reality?
