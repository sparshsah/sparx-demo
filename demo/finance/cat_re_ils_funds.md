# True Diversification via Cat/Re/ILS Funds? (WIP)

## Motivating Idea

- E.g. Local natural disasters
  can strike abruptly,
  but aren't necessarily
  too correlated
  with global
  growth/equity/credit or inflation
  cycles/crises.
  - Of course,
    if an asteroid impact
    triggered a megavolcano,
    blotting out the sun
    and plunging the Earth
    into a super ice age
    that set off a worldwide
    mass extinction event,
    that would probably show up
    in the economic numbers.
    But you get my point.
- So, getting paid a premium/coupon
  to bear the risk
  could be truly diversifying?

## Cautionary Tales

### What do AQR and PIMCO know that we don't?

- AQR
  - Tale:
    - launched AQR Re in this space around 2011
      - hired a bunch of industry veterans
      - invested a ton into spinning up operations
      - posted decent returns...
    - yet, shuttered the unit within 5 years (around 2015)
      - without a clear impetus
      - citing only a corporate-safe excuse
        about operational complexity and strategy capacity.
- Allianz/PIMCO
  - Tale:
    - launched their own ILS platform around 2019
      - *also* hired a bunch of industry veterans
      - *also* invested a ton into spinning up operations
      - *also* posted decent returns...
    - and yet, *also* shuttered their unit within 5 years (around 2023)
      - *also* without a clear impetus
      - *also* citing only a corporate-safe excuse
        about operational complexity and strategy capacity.
  - Notes:
    - The PIMCO ILS team went on to found King Ridge Capital,
      subadviser on the `TXSE:ILS` ETF from above.
    - Around 2024, Allianz/PIMCO relaunched
      two separate fresh reinsurance ventures.
      - Pando Re, where the cedant/sponsor (original insurer) is
        Aspen Insurance's casualty/liability business
        (so, in particular, *not* dealing with catastrophe/disaster).
      - Sconset Re, where the cedant/sponsor (original insurer) is
        Allianz Insurance's own life/annuity business
        (so, again, *not* catastrophe/disaster).

It's possible that
giant asset managers
whose major value prop
is synergies/economies of scale
from highly-marketable funds
running on shared firmwide
data/trading/reporting infrastructure
simply aren't effective hosts
for niche/opaque/finnicky investment strategies,
so both sides come to realize
they gain nothing from the tie-up
and hence part ways.
But it's also possible that
the big guys
are initially enthusiastic
but eventually get a sinking gut feel
as they observe the precarity
of picking up pennies
in front of a steamroller.
Which of these scenarios
is closer to reality?
